## A portfolio you can walk into

I wanted visitors to discover my work through a place that felt personal. A newspaper wrapped around two wada pavs became the starting point: what if unwrapping it revealed my contact information? That idea grew into a Mumbai street where a garage computer holds my projects, a chai stall offers blog posts as newspapers, and a lift presents my resume.

The challenge became making those experiences behave like one website: readable content, coherent navigation, direct links, responsive framing, and a reliable return to the street.

### My contribution

I owned the concept, visual references, interaction direction, task briefs, and acceptance decisions. I configured specialist AI review roles, directed revisions, and used the resulting models, code, and checks to evaluate the experience. AI assistants performed substantial modeling, JavaScript implementation, and asset integration.

My contribution was turning a personal creative idea into specific, reviewable work—and taking responsibility for the result I approved.

## The stack, and why it is split

The project combines a 3D navigation layer with normal web documents. A newspaper can be a physical object in the scene while its article remains selectable, scrollable HTML.

| Layer | Tools | Responsibility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interactive world | JavaScript ES modules, Three.js, WebGL | Scene graph, camera choreography, raycasting, materials, animation, and rendering effects |
| Asset production | Blender, glTF / GLB | Editable models, textures, named interaction anchors, and browser-ready exports |
| Written content | Astro, MDX, HTML/CSS, same-origin iframe | Project archive, readable articles, responsive layouts, and document navigation |
| Build and delivery | Vite, Sharp, Git/GitHub, Vercel configuration | Bundling, generated photo variants/manifests, versioning, and static deployment |

The implementation includes PostHog instrumentation for semantic actions such as opening a landmark or project. That gives the site a measurement foundation; I am not claiming an engagement improvement from this work.

## Three engineering problems worth explaining

### 1. Treating the camera as a shared resource

What happens when someone selects Contact halfway through the garage entrance animation? Two controllers cannot keep writing the same camera.

The route coordinator releases the previous experience and checks a navigation token after asynchronous work. Each destination manages its own transition state, suspends street input, and restores the saved view when it exits. Nested newspaper, counter, and photo-album routes share that street snapshot instead of repeatedly overwriting it.

This taught me to reason about **state, asynchronous races, cancellation, and ownership** together. A route token rejects stale continuations; a controller still has to stop its obsolete animations and release input.

### 2. Keeping the style without losing the information

The optional Pixel mode combines Three.js's pixelated render pass with output conversion and a color-quantization shader. Selected artwork is drawn sharply against a full-resolution depth prepass so signs stay legible while walls can still hide them.

This made **depth, render order, color handling, and device-pixel sizing** concrete. A sharp overlay drawn on top of everything would solve readability while breaking the world. Additional full-resolution passes also cost work, so a pixel look is not automatically a performance optimization.

### 3. Making a 3D asset behave like an interface

The garage monitor, contact counter, and lift depend on model structure, not just appearance. Named anchors, consistent scale, and local-to-world transforms connect Blender geometry to camera shots, moving parts, and projected HTML targets.

When the garage was enlarged and the neighboring stall moved, the runtime needed those shared transforms to stay aligned. Checks against actual exported geometry addressed screen corners, shutter poses, framing, and shop separation.

The newspaper added a useful geometry lesson. Early folds could satisfy simple bounds while looking like a boat or a box. The revised implementation uses one continuous sheet with art-directed deformation. Intermediate-pose review and topology/clearance checks answered different questions. It is a deliberate animation approximation, not a cloth simulator.

## How I delegated with AI

Usage limits pushed me to prepare before starting another generation cycle. I used the waiting time to inspect existing work and write Markdown handoffs: the goal, relevant references, what must stay, the allowed change, evidence required, and a stopping condition.

The specialist roles divided the review questions. The architect considered silhouette and placement; the set designer considered composition and believable supports; the Blender technician considered geometry, naming, materials, and export safety. The configured workflow gives a World Art Director role responsibility for consolidating findings into one brief, with one implementer owning edits to the Blender scene.

These were responsibilities assigned to AI assistants. I configured a development workflow around them; I did not build or train a new agent platform. Revisions used the relevant reviewers, and the worklog records interruptions as well as completed reviews. One garage pass continued in the main task after limits interrupted the technician handoff.

### A real handoff: please ground the biscuits

One saved revision brief addressed biscuits suspended inside the chai stall's jars. The task was small; its boundaries made it useful.

## Skills I developed through the project

| Skill | Where I practiced it |
| --- | --- |
| Creative direction and interaction design | Translating Mumbai memories into destinations, camera journeys, and discoverable objects |
| Technical communication and delegation | Writing bounded briefs, reconciling reviews, preserving successful work, and defining completion |
| Graphics and asset integration | Understanding transforms, depth, materials, exported anchors, and art-directed geometry |
| Asynchronous frontend design | Reasoning about camera ownership, route history, interrupted transitions, and stale results |
| Content and build pipelines | Separating Astro documents from WebGL, generating image variants, and keeping source assets distinct from served outputs |
| Validation and engineering judgment | Combining logic checks, independent export inspection, regression tests, and visual review |

My graphics background helped me recognize the underlying concepts. This project pushed me to apply them across a browser application—and to explain the decisions clearly enough that another assistant could act on them.

## What the evidence supports, and what comes next

The project records targeted checks for routing, camera restoration, geometry, rendering state, content builds, failure handling, and resource cleanup, alongside browser screenshots and reviews. Loading separates a two-worker asset queue from the ordered visual reveal; cleanup distinguishes owned GPU resources from shared ones.

Historical passing checks are useful evidence for those revisions. They do not establish universal device support or a current performance benchmark. Some later changes still have pending visual acceptance, and production deployment is tracked separately from local implementation.

My next priorities are a controlled performance pass on representative devices, broader physical-device and accessibility testing, and an accessible collection entry point for the walking characters. I would also keep tightening module boundaries as the number of destinations grows.

The outcome is both a personal world and a working lesson in engineering ownership: define the experience, divide the work, and inspect what actually reaches the browser.
