## Act I: Naturally, I Bought Two

Last year, while visiting India, I was on my way to my grandma's place when we stopped for wada pav.

Naturally, I bought two.

They came wrapped in newspaper. I ate the first one without giving the packaging much thought. But when I reached for the second, an advertisement caught my eye. I don't remember what it said. I remember that it stayed with me after the snack was gone.

A few months later, in a Mobile Game Development class, I found myself thinking about the personality packed into games like *EarthBound*, *A Link to the Past*, and *Streets of Rage*. Little sprites. Expressive worlds. Places you remembered.

I was studying Computer Science with a focus on Multimedia and Creative Technologies. I wanted to make something that put the *creative* in Creative Technologist.

And if I was going to build a little world about myself, where better to begin than Mumbai?

I grew up in its suburbs, surrounded by roadside stalls, rickshaws, crowded signs, chai breaks, and a thousand tiny things happening at once. I liked watching the details everyone else walked past.

My existing portfolio had an editorial, magazine-like feel. What if its pages also existed as places you could visit?

That's where the newspaper came back.

Instead of opening an ordinary Contact page, you could approach a wada pav stall and unwrap a newspaper to find my contact information. Obviously, I needed my own newspaper: **The Chai & Code Chronicle**. An entire publication mostly about me. Very modest.

One wrapper was becoming a neighborhood.

## Act II: The Five-Hour Waiting Room

I started in Canva, using a newspaper template, then brought the exported image into Blender as a texture. The plan was to make the paper fold, crumple, and unwrap around two wada pavs.

The newspaper had other plans.

I had beginner-level Blender experience. Getting the deformation to feel natural was beyond what I could manage, even after experimenting and asking people for help. The project entered that familiar folder: *I'll come back to this.*

In early September, I saw Astra working with Blender. I wasn't expecting a finished Mumbai street from one prompt, although that would have been convenient. I gathered references, drew rough sketches, and began working through individual buildings and stalls.

For the first time, I could see the place taking shape.

Then I hit my usage limit.

### A Budget Becomes a Brief

I called these interruptions the **five-hour token blackout**. Ten minutes earlier, I'd been obsessing over a model. Now I was waiting for my next turn.

I could have paid for a more expensive subscription. But I was working within my ChatGPT Plus budget, and I wasn't about to upgrade because a virtual chai stall was being difficult. I was already building Mumbai on the internet. I needed to draw the line somewhere.

So I used the waiting time to review, sketch, and write Markdown handoffs.

What should change? What should stay? Which references mattered? How would the result be checked?

I'd spent too many interactions explaining things halfway through making them. Writing them down forced me to notice decisions I hadn't actually made yet.

I learned to write better documentation because I was too stubborn to pay for more tokens.

### Learning to Delegate

Those documents became the basis for specialized AI roles: an architect reviewing proportions and placement, a set designer reviewing composition and believable props, and a Blender technician reviewing geometry, textures, and export constraints. One implementer could work from the combined brief.

One revision involved biscuits floating inside the chai stall's jars. The brief became specific: replace them with grounded golden stacks, leave room below the lids, clarify the glass, preserve the surrounding stall, and check both viewing angles before exporting.

“Make it look better” hadn't told anyone where to begin. This did.

The reviews gave me different ways to examine an asset. I still had to decide whether it felt like the Mumbai I remembered. A model could pass its export checks and still miss the point.

## Act III: A Street Needs People

Individual Blender models still weren't a website. I needed a way to assemble them, navigate between them, and make their interactions feel connected.

I'd never worked with Three.js before, although graphics and game-development experience gave me somewhere to start. Exporting GLB files became the bridge: geometry, materials, and textures packaged for the browser.

AI handled a substantial amount of the coding and asset integration. I defined the experience, supplied references and constraints, reviewed what appeared, and directed changes. I wanted usable navigation, readable content, responsive layouts, and evidence that an interaction worked beyond the model saying it did.

The newspaper eventually unrolled into Contact. The garage monitor opened my project archive. The chai stall's newspapers became entry points to actual articles. Returning from each experience needed to feel like stepping back onto the same street.

Showing friends the project helped me think about how it felt to explore. And eventually I noticed something fairly obvious.

Where were the people?

So I asked friends whether they'd like to become walking pixel characters. A perfectly normal request.

Their photographs became references for illustrated sprites. But I wanted visitors to discover something about each person, too. That brought back another childhood memory: the cricket trading cards I collected in little packs for around fifty rupees. I still have the folder.

Clicking a friend could open a card. I asked them to choose a movie they liked, and those choices became part of the experience. Familiar faces and small personal details gave the street a reason to feel inhabited.

None of this arrived as a complete plan. Each finished piece suggested the next one. A wrapper led to a newspaper; the newspaper needed a stall; the street needed my friends.

AI helped me build far more of it than I could have managed alone in the same time. It also made ownership harder to dodge. If I approved an output and put my name on it, I was responsible for what visitors experienced.

The tools could help construct the world. They couldn't decide which memories belonged in it.

I started with two wada pavs. Somewhere along the way, I found a way to bring more of myself into the work.
